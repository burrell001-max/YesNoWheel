How to Solve the "What Should I Eat?" Problem

Last updated: November 18, 2026

You are hungry, nothing sounds good and every option feels wrong. This routine turns "I do not know" into a meal in about five minutes.

Why it is so hard

Most of us make several hundred small food decisions a week, and each one costs a little attention. By the time you are hungry you may already be tired from other choices, so even a simple question like "What do I want?" feels heavy. Psychologists call this decision fatigue, and it is one reason people end up grazing on snacks, ordering the same takeaway or skipping a meal altogether.

A second problem is the size of the menu. When you can eat anything, you compare everything, and the more you compare, the more each option seems to have a flaw. The fix is not to find a perfect meal. It is to make the question smaller.

Start with what your body is asking for

Before you open the fridge, check in for ten seconds. Are you actually hungry, or are you bored, thirsty or tired? If it has been a few hours since you ate, you are probably hungry. If not, a glass of water and a short walk may tell you something. Then ask three quick questions:

  • Hot or cold? Warm food comforts, cold food refreshes.
  • Light or filling? Think about how hungry you really are and what you have to do next.
  • Crunchy, creamy or brothy? Texture is often the thing you are really craving.

Those three answers cut the list from hundreds of meals to a handful.

Shrink the list

Choose a type first, such as soup, noodles, eggs, rice bowls or a salad with something hearty on top. Then list three meals in that type that you can make or order within twenty minutes. Setting a time limit matters because it removes the dishes that sound good but take too long, which is a common reason people give up and snack instead.

If you cook at home, look at what needs using up first. A wilting bunch of greens, half an onion and a few eggs is already a meal. Starting from ingredients you have, rather than a recipe you wish you had ingredients for, avoids a trip to the store.

Keep a go-to list

The most useful habit is also the simplest: write down ten meals you genuinely enjoy and keep the list in your phone. Include a mix, such as three that take ten minutes or less, three you can make from pantry staples, two you would order out and two that are a little special. When you are stuck, you pick from your own list instead of starting from nothing.

Review the list every month or so. Cross off anything you have gotten tired of and add something new you tried and liked. A list that changes slowly stays interesting without making you think about it every day.

Stock a small safety net

Decisions get easier when the basics are on hand. A few staples cover a lot of nights: pasta or rice, canned beans or tuna, eggs, frozen vegetables, a jar of good sauce and something to make it taste like you meant it, such as garlic, lemon or cheese. With those, "nothing in the house" is rarely true.

Spin and go

When you still cannot choose, put your short list on a wheel, such as our food picker wheel, and spin it. The wheel is not there to be bossy. It ends the loop of comparing and gives you something to react to. If the result excites you, cook it. If you feel a small pang of disappointment, that is useful information too: it tells you what you actually wanted, so make that instead.

Whatever you choose, decide before you start looking. Standing in front of an open fridge for five minutes is the most common way to end up with a bowl of cereal for dinner.

Quick recap

  • Check whether you are really hungry.
  • Pick hot or cold, light or filling, and a texture.
  • Choose a type and three options you can make in twenty minutes.
  • Keep a go-to list of ten meals and a few pantry staples.
  • Spin the wheel and notice how you feel about the result.
