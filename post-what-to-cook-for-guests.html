What to Cook for Guests When You Can't Decide

Last updated: November 18, 2026

Guests arrive in two hours and you have no plan. Narrow it down, cook what cooperates, and let a wheel make the final call.

Start with the people

The best menu starts with who is coming. A few minutes of questions up front saves a lot of stress later.

  • Ask about allergies and diets. The most common food allergens are milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy and sesame. Vegetarian, vegan, halal, kosher and gluten-free guests are also common. A quick text a few days before works well.
  • Count heads. The number of people decides whether you can cook one big pot or need several dishes.
  • Think about the occasion. A casual weeknight meal, a birthday and a holiday dinner call for different levels of effort.
  • Consider your own time. You want to enjoy your guests, not spend the evening in the kitchen.

Dishes that cooperate

Choose food that does most of the work for you. Good options include:

  • Make-ahead mains such as braises, stews, chilis and lasagna, which often taste better the next day.
  • Sheet-pan meals that roast together with minimal cleanup.
  • Build-your-own meals like tacos, grain bowls or a pasta bar. They handle different diets easily, because each guest builds a plate.
  • Slow-cooker or oven dishes that you can leave alone while you greet people.

Avoid dishes that must be cooked at the last minute in several pans. Aim for no more than one item that needs your attention after guests arrive.

Build a simple menu

A reliable shape is a starter, a main, one or two sides and a dessert. You do not need to cook all of it. A cheese or vegetable platter can be the starter, and good ice cream with fruit can be the dessert. Try only one new recipe, and keep the rest familiar so that the evening does not depend on an untested dish.

Work backward from serving time. Write down when each thing needs to start, and what can be done the day before, such as chopping vegetables, making a dressing or baking dessert.

How much to make

As a rough guide, plan about six to eight ounces (170 to 225 grams) of raw boneless meat or fish per person for a main course, and about a cup of cooked sides per person for each side dish. For appetizers before a meal, four to six small bites per person is typical. It is normal to have some leftovers, and it is better than running out.

Food safety

Keep hot food hot, above 140 degrees Fahrenheit (60 degrees Celsius), and cold food cold, below 40 degrees Fahrenheit (4 degrees Celsius). Do not leave perishable food out for more than two hours, or one hour in hot weather above 90 degrees Fahrenheit (32 degrees Celsius). Cook poultry to an internal temperature of 165 degrees Fahrenheit (74 degrees Celsius), and use a thermometer instead of guessing.

Build a short list

Write down three to five dishes that fit your guests, your budget and your time. For each, ask: Can I make it ahead? Do I have most of the ingredients? Does it fit everyone's diets? Cross off anything that fails two of the three.

Set the table and the mood

Small touches make a simple meal feel special. Set the table before anyone arrives, put out water and glasses, and light a few candles or play quiet music. Offer a non-alcoholic drink alongside any wine or beer, so every guest has a good option. Keep a clean space ready for coats and bags, and give guests a small job, such as pouring drinks, if they offer to help. Clearing up is easier when you start with an empty dishwasher and a bin for scraps close to the sink.

Let the wheel pick

If two or three dishes still feel equal, put them on a wheel and spin. It cuts the dithering, and your reaction to the result will tell you which one you really wanted. You can also use a wheel to choose a side dish, a dessert or the evening's music.

Finally, remember that guests come for company as much as food. A relaxed host and a simple meal beat a stressed host with an elaborate one every time.
