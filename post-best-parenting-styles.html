What Are the Four Parenting Styles?

Last updated: November 18, 2026

Psychologists describe four parenting styles. Knowing them helps you spot habits worth keeping and ones worth changing, without turning into a label.

Where the four styles come from

In the 1960s, developmental psychologist Diana Baumrind described patterns in how parents balance two things: warmth and responsiveness on one side, and rules and expectations on the other. In the 1980s, Eleanor Maccoby and John Martin refined her work into the four styles most people refer to today. They are descriptions of tendencies, not scores, and most families do not fit neatly into one box.

The four styles

Authoritative

High warmth and clear expectations. These parents set limits and explain them, listen to the child's point of view, and expect age-appropriate responsibility. A typical response to a missed homework assignment: "I know you were tired. What is your plan to catch up, and how can I help?"

Authoritarian

High control and low warmth. Rules are strict, reasons are rarely given, and obedience matters most. A typical response: "Because I said so." Punishment tends to come first and conversation second.

Permissive

High warmth and few limits. These parents are loving and want to avoid conflict, so rules are loose or inconsistently enforced. A typical response: letting a bedtime slide again because the child is upset.

Uninvolved

Low warmth and low expectations. Basic needs may be met, but there is little guidance or emotional connection. This pattern often reflects stress, overload or a parent's own difficulties rather than a lack of caring.

What the research suggests

Across many studies, children raised with an authoritative approach tend, on average, to do better on measures such as school performance, self-regulation and social skills. Authoritarian parenting is more often linked to anxiety and lower self-esteem, and permissive parenting to difficulty with limits and self-control.

These findings need to be read carefully. They describe averages, the effects are modest, and the research is mostly correlational, so it cannot prove that a style causes an outcome. Children also shape their parents: a child with an intense temperament may pull a parent toward stricter or more flexible habits. Culture and context matter too. Firmer, more directive parenting can carry a different meaning, and different outcomes, in communities where it is the norm and is delivered with warmth.

Be firm and kind together

The practical lesson from the research is that warmth and structure work together instead of competing. A few habits make this concrete:

  • Explain the reason behind a rule. Even a short "Screens go off at eight so your brain can wind down" helps children follow it.
  • Offer limited choices. "Do you want to brush your teeth before or after the story?" keeps the limit and gives the child some control.
  • Name the feeling and hold the line. "You are disappointed and the answer is still no" validates without giving in.
  • Follow through calmly. Consistent, predictable consequences teach more than loud ones.
  • Repair after conflict. Saying "I was sharper than I meant to be" models the skill you want your child to learn.

You are not a label

Most parents move between styles depending on the day, the child and how much sleep they got. You may be warm and firm about homework, then permissive at the end of a long week. That is normal. The goal is not to be perfect but to notice which way you lean when you are stressed, and to steer back toward connection plus clear expectations.

If you recognize a pattern you would like to change, pick one situation, such as mornings or bedtime, and try one new habit for two weeks. Small, specific changes stick better than a resolution to become a different parent. If parenting feels overwhelming, or you are worried about your child's behavior or mood, a pediatrician, school counselor or family therapist can help.

This article is general information, not professional advice. Sources: Baumrind, "Current patterns of parental authority," Developmental Psychology Monograph, 1971; Maccoby and Martin, "Socialization in the context of the family," in the Handbook of Child Psychology, 1983.
