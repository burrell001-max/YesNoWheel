Types of Keto Diet and How to Get Started Safely

Last updated: November 18, 2026

Keto is a very low-carb, high-fat way of eating. Here are the main versions, how to begin, and who should check with a doctor first.

What keto is

A ketogenic diet cuts carbohydrates sharply and replaces most of those calories with fat. With little glucose available, the body starts to make ketones from fat for fuel, a state called ketosis. It usually takes a few days of eating very few carbs to get there. The diet was developed in the 1920s as a medical treatment for epilepsy, and it is still used that way under supervision. It later became popular as a weight-loss approach.

The four common types

Standard ketogenic diet

The most common version. A typical split is about 70 to 75 percent of calories from fat, about 20 percent from protein and 5 to 10 percent from carbohydrates, which often works out to roughly 20 to 50 grams of net carbs per day. Net carbs are total carbs minus fiber.

Cyclical ketogenic diet

A pattern such as five keto days followed by two higher-carb days. Some athletes use it to refill energy stores, but it is harder to follow and the evidence is limited.

Targeted ketogenic diet

A standard plan with a small amount of carbohydrate eaten around workouts. It is mostly used by people who train hard and want more energy during exercise.

High-protein ketogenic diet

Similar to the standard plan with more protein, for example about 60 percent fat, 35 percent protein and 5 percent carbs. It may be easier to stick to and more filling for some people.

Getting started

  1. Talk to a professional first if you have a medical condition or take medication (see below).
  2. Learn the foods. Typical choices are eggs, fish, meat, poultry, non-starchy vegetables like spinach, broccoli and zucchini, avocado, olive oil, nuts, seeds, butter and cheese.
  3. Know what to limit. Bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, sugar, most fruit, sweet drinks and many sauces are high in carbs.
  4. Track your carbs for the first couple of weeks. A food-tracking app helps you learn what is in your meals.
  5. Plan your meals so that you are not caught hungry with nothing on hand. A short list of go-to breakfasts, lunches and dinners works better than starting from scratch each day.
  6. Drink water and watch your salt. Low-carb eating makes the body shed water and sodium, which can cause headaches, dizziness and fatigue.

What to expect early on

Many people report symptoms during the first days or weeks, often called the "keto flu": headache, tiredness, irritability, nausea or muscle cramps. These usually ease as the body adapts, and drinking enough fluid, getting enough sodium and eating enough food can help. If symptoms are severe or do not improve, speak to a doctor. Changes in digestion are also common, so include fiber-rich low-carb vegetables, seeds and nuts.

Who should be careful

A ketogenic diet is not suitable for everyone, and it can be risky for some people. Speak to your doctor before starting if you:

  • have type 1 or type 2 diabetes, especially if you take insulin or certain medications, as blood sugar and ketone levels need medical monitoring;
  • are pregnant or breastfeeding;
  • have kidney disease, liver disease, pancreatitis or gallbladder problems;
  • take medication for blood pressure, as doses may need adjusting as you lose weight;
  • have, or have had, an eating disorder;
  • are planning to put a child on a restrictive diet, which should only happen under medical supervision.

Think long term

Many people lose weight quickly at first, partly from water. The longer-term picture is less clear. Studies comparing keto with other balanced diets often find similar results after a year, and the best diet is generally the one you can maintain. Some people see their LDL cholesterol rise on keto, so blood tests are worth doing. Very low carb intake can also make it harder to get enough fiber, vitamins and minerals, so variety matters.

Before you commit, ask yourself whether you can keep eating this way at restaurants, on holiday and at family meals. If not, a moderate, lower-carb approach may be more realistic. A registered dietitian can help you tailor a plan to your health and your life.

This article is general information, not medical advice. Talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before making major changes to your diet.
