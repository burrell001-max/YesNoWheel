How to Split Chores and Seats Fairly With a Wheel

Last updated: November 25, 2026

By Kent B. Williams

Random assignment does not make chores fun. It does remove the advantage held by whoever is best at arguing, which is usually the whole problem.

Try it

Open the name picker. Put the jobs on the wheel, then run the names separately, so the pairing is drawn rather than negotiated.

Why a wheel helps with housework

In most shared houses the same person ends up volunteering, or the same person ends up not. Both are a kind of tax on being reasonable. A visible random draw moves the conversation from "who deserves a break this week" to "what did the draw say", and that conversation is a short one.

The wheel does not fix an unfair split. It allocates jobs fairly once you have agreed what the jobs are, and those are two separate arguments.

Step 1: Agree the job list first

Write the jobs at a similar size. "Clean the kitchen" and "take the bins out" are not equal, so bundling matters more than the draw ever will.

Group them into bundles that take roughly the same time and effort. Four people, four bundles: bins and hallway, kitchen and bathroom, vacuum and dusting, laundry and bins. Unequal bundles produce a draw that is technically random and practically unfair, and everyone knows it.

If a bundle is much worse than the others, rotate it separately rather than pretending the draw fixed it.

Step 2: Decide who gets what

Two methods, both using elimination so nobody is picked twice and nobody is left out.

  • People on the wheel, one job at a time. Spin for the kitchen, remove that person, spin for the bathroom, and so on. Best when the jobs are already agreed and you want it over quickly.
  • Jobs on the wheel, one person at a time. Spin for each person in turn. Slower, more dramatic, better with children who want to watch their own name come up.

Either way, remove each winner as you go. Without elimination you will get the same person twice and the same person never, which is the one outcome that makes a draw look rigged.

Step 3: Set the swap window

Allow swaps for ten minutes after the draw, by mutual agreement, then close it. Swaps are fine and often sensible: someone has a bad back, someone is out on Thursday.

What is not fine is a silent trade three days later, because that is how the draw stops meaning anything. Write the swap down: a note on the fridge or a message in the group chat.

If one person swaps out of a job every single week, that is not a swap. That is the draw being overruled by negotiation, and it is worth naming.

Step 4: Rotate, do not redraw everything

The point of a draw is a fair starting order, not a fresh argument every Sunday. Draw once at the start, then rotate weekly.

Use the wheel again when something changes: someone moves out, a new job appears, the rotation has drifted, or the bundles change. Not because someone did not like their job this week.

Save the list and copy the share link so the same draw can be repeated on another device or found again after the browser is cleared.

Seats, desks and car places

The same method works for anything that gets allocated and argued about.

  • Car seats. Put the seats on the wheel, one spin per person, remove as you go. Rotating the front seat weekly is the fairest version; a wheel makes the rotation visible.
  • Cinema seats. Let the wheel pick the block or row, then let people sort themselves out inside it. The argument is usually about the middle, not the seat.
  • Desks. Draw at the start of term, then rotate monthly. Windows, radiators and doors are the real prizes, so draw for those positions specifically.
  • Dinner table. Draw who sits next to whom rather than where. It solves the "I always get the corner" complaint and it makes the meal better.
  • Parking spaces. Number them, then spin weekly. Cheap, visible, and nobody has to be the bad guy.

For anything you repeat, keep the list and the order you drew in. If you need to reconstruct why someone has the good spot, the order is the answer.

Children and the "it is not fair" problem

The most useful sentence is: the wheel does not have favourites, and it does not remember last time. Say it, then prove it by spinning where they can see the list before and the result after.

A draw a child watched is much harder to argue with than one you report. That is not manipulation; it is the same reason adults accept visible draws and resent quiet ones.

If a child is genuinely upset, fix the job rather than the draw. A shorter version of a job they can actually do is fairer than re-spinning until they get the easy one, which teaches exactly the wrong lesson about both fairness and luck.

The limit worth stating plainly

A wheel can allocate jobs. It cannot decide whether the split is fair in the first place. If one person is doing most of the work, the answer is not a better random draw, it is a different list.

Use the wheel for the part it is good at: removing the negotiation advantage from a list everyone already agreed. Then go and do the job.
