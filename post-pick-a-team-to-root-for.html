How to Pick a Team to Root For (and Stick With It)

Last updated: November 25, 2026

By Kent B. Williams

Fandom usually arrives by accident: a hometown, a parent's team, a season that happened to be on television when you were nine. If none of that applies, a draw is a faster start than another evening of reading previews.

The wheel cannot make you care about a club. What it can do is end the comparing and give you a name to start from. Everything after that is a routine, and the routine is built in the first month.

Try it

Open the NFL team wheel. Draw a team in elimination mode, then give it the first month described below: three full games and one rival.

Why a random team is a reasonable start

People who move country, or who come to a sport late, do not get the accident. They get a shortlist of clubs that mean nothing to them yet and a choice that feels bigger than it is.

A draw helps in three ways. It stops the comparison, which could otherwise run for weeks. It produces a starting point you can act on this weekend. And it takes the choice outside your own taste, which is worth doing if your taste is the reason you have been circling for three seasons.

Decide what kind of fan you want to be

Before you build the list, decide how much of your week the sport can have. A person who will watch two games a month and a person who will watch every game need different teams, or at least different expectations of themselves.

Three questions are enough to settle it:

  • How many games will you watch in a normal week, and can you actually see them, on a subscription or free to air?
  • Do you want a team close enough to visit, or is distance irrelevant?
  • Do you want a team that is already good, or a project you can follow while it builds?

There are no wrong answers, but they change the list. A team whose games you can never watch is a team you will quietly drop, however much you liked the badge on the day of the draw.

Cut the list before you spin

Thirty teams is too many to hold in your head, and a result you cannot check against the rest of the list is a result you argue with. Cut to a working list first, and cut for the boring reasons rather than the romantic ones.

Remove the clubs you already dislike, including the one a friend supports. That sounds petty until you are three months in and cannot explain why you dread the fixture.

Remove any team you would only follow because it is winning. Ask what you would think of it in a bad season, and if the honest answer is nothing, it belongs off the list.

Keep the rest. Six to ten is plenty: a shorter list is easier to accept because every name on it is one you would genuinely take.

Set the rules before the spin

Two rules are worth agreeing with yourself, or with the group, before the wheel turns.

First: the result stands for a season. Not forever, and not for a decade. A season is long enough to find out whether you enjoy the games and short enough that a mistake costs months rather than years.

Second: one draw, one team. Respinning because you hoped for something else is the same as not using the wheel at all. If the result disappoints you, that is worth noticing, because it usually means the team you wanted was already on the list and you did not need a draw to find it.

Write both rules down, in the group chat or in a note you will see again. A rule announced out loud is much easier to keep than one you meant to remember.

What to do in the first month

A club becomes familiar through specifics, not through statements of loyalty.

Watch three games properly rather than checking the score afterwards. Pick two or three players and learn what they do, so the game stops being a blur of strangers in the same shirt.

Learn the shape of the season: how many games there are, when the playoffs start, which competition the club cares about most. This is the difference between following a team and glancing at one.

Choose a rival. Almost any reason will do, from a friend's team to a result you did not enjoy, and having one gives an ordinary midweek game something at stake.

Find one person to watch with, in the room or on a call. Sport is a social habit before it is anything else, and a draw that nobody else knows about rarely survives a busy month.

Group draws, sweepstakes and draft orders

The team wheels do three different jobs, and mixing them up causes most of the arguments.

For a sweepstake, the team wheel is exactly right: one spin per person, each team removed once it is drawn, and a note of who got what. That note is what prevents a dispute when the final arrives and somebody claims they never agreed to anything.

For a draft order, the team wheels are the wrong tool. A draft needs an order of people, not a list of clubs, so put the managers' names on the name picker and use elimination so each manager is placed once.

For an office or family "who do we support" draw, take one league at a time. Handing somebody an MLB team when they were only ever going to watch basketball is how a fun draw turns into a chore by November.

When switching is fair

A draw is a starting point, and some starting points do not work out. That is not a failure of the method, it is information you did not have before the season started.

Switching is fair when you have given it a month of proper attention and still find yourself not watching. It is less fair when the team is simply losing, because losing is the part you signed up for in advance.

If you do switch, say so once and then move on. Quietly drifting from club to club is worse than an honest change of mind, because it leaves you supporting nobody and owning a drawer of merchandise for teams you no longer check.

One clean switch is easier to explain to the people you watch with than a slow fade, and it keeps the draw useful: it narrowed the field once, and it can do it again next season.

The two-minute version

Ask how many games you will watch and whether you can actually see them. Remove the clubs you dislike and the ones you would only want in a good year.

  • Put the rest on the wheel, six to ten names.
  • Agree that the result lasts one season.
  • Spin once, then write the name down somewhere you will see it.
  • Learn three players, watch three full games, pick a rival.

By the end of the month you will know whether it worked. That is a fact about you rather than about the wheel, and it is the part no amount of reading can give you.
