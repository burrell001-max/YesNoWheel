How to Use a Yes or No Wheel to Make a Decision

Last updated: November 25, 2026

By Kent B. Williams

A yes or no wheel does not know more than you do. What it does is end the loop: the ten minutes you spend reopening the same question. Here is a routine that takes about two minutes and leaves you with an answer you will actually act on.

Try it

Open the Yes or No wheel. Type the question into the banner, say both answers out loud, then spin once and keep the result.

What the wheel is good at, and what it is not

The wheel earns its keep on decisions that are low-stakes, roughly equal, and reversible. Lunch. Whether to go out. Which of three chores to start with. Whose turn it is. On choices like those, the value is not the randomness. It is the deadline. A spin forces an answer, and your reaction to that answer tells you something you could not reach by thinking in circles.

It is the wrong tool for anything where missing information is the real problem. A purchase you have not priced. A medical question. A contract. Any decision another person has to agree to. A wheel can settle your side of a conversation. It cannot settle theirs.

Step 1: Write the question so it has two answers

Three rules: one decision, one action, one time frame. If your question cannot be answered yes or no, it is not ready for the wheel yet.

  • Too vague: "Should I sort out my room?" Spin-ready: "Should I spend 20 minutes tidying the desk before dinner?"
  • Too vague: "Should I go out tonight?" Spin-ready: "Should I go to the party for one hour tonight?"
  • Too vague: "Should I reply to that email?" Spin-ready: "Should I reply to the landlord before I leave the house?"
  • Too vague: "Should I buy it?" Spin-ready: "Should I buy the secondhand bike for $120 this weekend?"
  • Too vague: "Should I take a break?" Spin-ready: "Should I stop working at 4pm and go for a walk?"

One test before you spin: imagine the answer arriving, and notice your next thought. If it is "but what about...", your question had a hidden third option. Either put that option into the question, or accept that the thing you are actually deciding is something else.

Step 2: Say both answers out loud before you spin

"Yes, I am going." "No, I am staying in." Say each one and watch what happens. One of them usually produces an immediate reaction: relief, or a small sinking feeling. That reaction is the answer you already had. The wheel did not supply it; it gave you a moment where the choice was real enough to feel.

This is why the spin is still worth doing when you suspect you have decided. You are not asking the wheel to be wise. You are closing the argument.

Step 3: Decide whether you need a third option

Switching the wheel from two answers to Yes, No and Maybe is the right call when the honest answer is "not yet" or "I need one more fact". It is the wrong call when Maybe is a place to hide. If you know you would never act on Maybe, leave it off; a third slice you will not use just makes the result easier to argue with.

If you do use it, decide in advance what Maybe means. "Maybe means I ask one question before deciding." "Maybe means I check the price, then spin again tomorrow." A Maybe with no rule attached is the same as not deciding, with extra steps.

Step 4: Spin once, then move

One spin. Every spin draws a fresh random number, so the wheel has no memory of the last result and is not balancing anything out. A run of Nos does not make a Yes more likely. A second spin is a new answer, not a confirmation of the first.

Adopt a one-respin rule and be honest about why you used it. Respinning because the result exposed an option you forgot is fine. Respinning because you did not like the answer is not a decision method; it is a search for permission, and you will keep going until you get the answer you wanted. If that is what is happening, put the wheel down and just do the thing you were hoping for.

Step 5: If it lands on the answer you did not want

There are three legitimate responses, and one that is not.

  • Accept it. Notice how fast the resistance fades once the question is closed. For most low-stakes decisions, keeping the question open costs more than getting it wrong.
  • Rewrite the question. A bad result often means the question was too broad. "Should I go out?" becomes "Should I go for an hour?" The narrower version is usually the real decision.
  • Add the missing option. If the wheel lands somewhere and your first thought is "actually, what I want is...", that option was never on the wheel. Put it on and spin again. That is not cheating; it is finishing the setup you skipped.

The one response that does not work: spinning until Yes. That is not the wheel deciding. That is you deciding and asking for cover, which is fine, but you can skip the ceremony.

Where the wheel earns a permanent spot

Some decisions repeat. Who cooks on Tuesday. Where to order from on Friday. Which of the four weekend jobs to start with. Build the list once and keep it: the custom wheel saves your entries in the browser, and a share link recreates the same wheel on another device. Nobody has to relitigate the shortlist every week; the wheel only has to handle the tie.

The results counter is the underrated part. Spin the same wheel five times and look at the spread. If one option keeps coming up, that is worth knowing. If you keep hoping a particular option does not come up, that is worth knowing too.

When to put the wheel down

  • Health, money you cannot afford to lose, legal or safety questions.
  • Decisions where the options are not comparable, so a random pick is not fair to any of them.
  • Anything that needs another person's agreement. A wheel settles your side only.
  • Draws that need a record, such as a prize with rules or a workplace allocation. Those need a documented process, not a screen.

The line is simple. If you would be fine either way and you just need to stop circling, spin. If getting it wrong would cost real money, health or trust, the wheel is entertainment, not a method.

Questions people ask

Is the result really random? Each spin draws a new random number, so no answer is "due". A run of five Nos is not the wheel correcting itself, and it does not make the next Yes more likely.

Does it remember my list? The custom wheel keeps your entries in your browser. A share link recreates the wheel on another device. There is no account and nothing to sign up for.

Can I use it with a group? Yes, and it is often better that way. One visible spin is harder to argue with than somebody saying "I do not mind, you pick". Add one option per person and let the wheel choose.

How do I avoid repeats in a group? Switch the custom wheel to elimination mode and remove each winner as you go. Four people, four jobs, four different answers.
