How to Pick a Restaurant When Nobody Can Agree

Last updated: November 25, 2026

The argument is almost never about food. It is about constraints nobody said out loud: budget, travel time, dietary needs, and who is too tired to queue.

Step 1: Say the constraints out loud

Constraints are facts, not opinions, and they cut the list rather than rank it. Get them on the table before anyone names a restaurant, because a place that breaks a constraint is not a candidate no matter how good it is.

  • Money. A rough number per person, including drinks and tip. "Cheap" means different things to different people at the same table.
  • Travel. Fifteen minutes on foot, or a drive with parking, are different decisions. Someone may have a bus to catch.
  • Dietary needs. Allergies and restrictions are constraints; preferences are not. Treat the difference seriously and the rest of the conversation gets easier.
  • Time. Kitchen closing, a booking, a babysitter, an early start. The latest possible sitting time is often the real decision.
  • The one must. Somebody needs a quiet table, or a high chair, or a screen showing the match. Ask before the pool is built, not after it wins.

A note on a phone is enough, as long as everyone can see the same five lines while the shortlist is being built.

Step 2: Agree what you are choosing

"Thai or Italian" is a different decision from "the place on 5th or the place by the station". Pick one level and stay on it. Mixing cuisines with venues on a single wheel is how groups end up spinning for "Italian", then arguing about which Italian place.

If the group genuinely has no shared pool, choose the cuisine first (one wheel), then the restaurant within it (a second wheel). Two spins, two small decisions, no argument about the category.

Step 3: Build a pool that survives the constraints

Go round the table and let each person put forward two places they would genuinely go to tonight. Then take out the ones that fail a constraint and stop at four to six survivors. Checking a list is mechanical; deciding who is allowed to add to it is the part that needs managing.

This is the step most groups skip, and it is the reason they stay stuck. A pool of "anywhere" means the spin picks a category rather than a place, and the decision restarts the moment the wheel stops.

Someone will name the place they always name. Let it in once, and if it has already lost twice this month, that is what a written "never again" list is for.

Write each option the way you would say it out loud: the name, roughly where it is, and a price. If you cannot say it in one breath, it is not ready.

Step 4: Spend the vetoes, then spin in front of everyone

Give each person one veto, used now, or agree a shared "never again" list before the pool is built. A veto exercised after the result is no longer a veto, it is a negotiation, and the person who suggested the place has already lost it.

With a large group, cap it: one strike each, and no strikes on your own suggestion. Otherwise the pool shrinks to nothing and you are back to "anywhere".

Then spin on one device that everybody can see. A result the table watched does not need reporting, and it does not need defending either. The food picker starts with sixteen dishes; replace them with your places in the text view, or keep the dishes for a night in.

If the result is unacceptable

Do not respin immediately. Ask what is wrong with it, and expect one of three answers.

  • A constraint was missing. The winner is forty minutes away and someone has an early start. Add the constraint, remove the option, spin once more.
  • An option is stale. The place closed, changed hands or stopped doing the thing you liked. Remove it permanently.
  • Someone had a preference they did not say. This is the useful case. Name it, and if the group agrees, that is the answer. No wheel required.

If it happens twice in a row, the group does not have a decision problem. It has an unmade agreement, usually about whether anyone actually wants to go out.

What to actually say, in order

Four sentences, in this order, get most groups to a table:

  • "What can we not do tonight?" Budget, travel, allergies, time. Write the answers where everyone can see them.
  • "Are we picking a cuisine or a place?" One level, not both.
  • "Name two you would actually go to." Then read the list back minus the ones that broke a rule.
  • "Last chance to veto, then we spin." Spin once, and leave.

One rule makes it stick: whoever complains after the spin has to name the option they wanted. That answer usually turns out to be the plan, and the wheel has still done its job by getting it out of them.

Delivery is a different list

Ordering in has its own constraints: delivery time, minimum order, and what survives the journey. Keep a separate saved wheel for delivery nights, and use elimination mode so the same place does not win three Fridays running.

Two lists, not one: the places you would go to, and the places that deliver well. A good restaurant is often a poor takeaway, and a shortlist that mixes them produces a winner nobody wanted.

Decide the delivery deadline as well. A forty-minute wait at half past eight is a different decision from the same food at six, and the budget is easier to hold before the app is open than after the total appears.

Keep the list for next time

Save the shortlist in the picker and put the share link where the group already argues: the chat, a pinned message, a note on the fridge. The list is the part worth keeping; the spin takes five seconds and can be repeated whenever the plan changes.

If you eat out often, keep two lists: one for "quick and close" and one for "worth the trip". The two rarely overlap, and mixing them is what makes the choice feel impossible.
