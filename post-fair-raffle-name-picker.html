How to Run a Fair Raffle or Prize Draw With a Name Picker

Last updated: November 25, 2026

By Kent B. Williams

A draw is fair when everyone can see the same list before the spin and the result is recorded after it. Everything else is detail.

Try it

Open the name picker. Load the entrant list, switch on elimination mode, and spin once with the screen visible to the room.

What fairness actually requires

Strip it down and a fair draw needs five things:

  • Every eligible person enters exactly once, unless the rules say otherwise.
  • The list is complete before the draw starts.
  • The selection is a single random pick from that list.
  • The result is recorded.
  • The redraw rule is agreed before anyone sees a name.

A browser wheel can do the third item very well. The other four are on the person running the draw, and they are where most disputes start.

Step 1: Build the entry list

One line per entry, exact names, no duplicates unless multiple entries are part of the rules. If the rules allow extra entries, say so out loud: "three entries per donation" is a rule; "three entries because the name was typed three times" is a mistake, and it is the mistake people notice.

Paste from a spreadsheet or a form export rather than typing. The name picker imports a list and lets you edit it in the text view, so a correction costs a few seconds instead of a re-entry.

If entries come from more than one place, combine them in one list, not in three separate spins. Two draws from two lists is not the same as one draw from the combined list, and the difference is exactly the kind of thing a winner's friend will spot.

Step 2: Check the count before you spin

Count the entries on screen and compare with the number you expect: the number of people who entered, plus any permitted bonus entries.

This single check catches most problems. A name typed twice, a name missing, a nickname instead of a full name, a duplicate from a copy-paste that ran one line too far. Fix the list while nobody has seen a result, because after the spin every correction looks like a fix.

Two habits do most of the work. Sort the list alphabetically and read it for the same name twice, then count the lines and compare with the entry count you wrote down. Typos collect at the edges of a paste, so read the first few and last few entries properly rather than trusting the middle.

Step 3: Draw where people can see it

Shared screen, projector, or a phone held up so the room can watch. If the entrants are remote, screen-record the draw and post the video.

The point is not that anyone suspects you. It is that a visible draw does not need defending afterwards, and you will not have to write a long explanation in the group chat at eleven at night.

What is on screen matters as much as where it is. Put the entry list and the winning name in the same frame: a screenshot of a winner with no visible list proves nothing, and it is the shot people ask for later. If you are drawing on a phone, turn the screen towards the room before you press spin, not after.

Step 4: Agree the redraw rule first

There is one good reason to redraw: the winner is not eligible. Age, location, staff membership, an employee's family, someone who has already won, someone who cannot claim the prize. Say which conditions count before the spin.

There is one bad reason: not liking the winner. A redraw because the "wrong" person won is not a redraw. It is a draw that has been overruled, and everyone can tell, which usually costs more goodwill than the prize was worth.

Also decide what happens if a winner does not respond. A deadline ("48 hours, then we redraw") written down in advance saves the awkward follow-up.

Step 5: Record the result

Screenshot the wheel with the winner visible, and keep the entry list. Note the date, the time and the number of entries. For a small giveaway that is plenty.

If money is involved, or the prize comes from a sponsor, or the draw has an audience, write the rules down before the draw: who is eligible, the entry period, one prize per person, how the winner is contacted, and what happens if they do not reply. That document is worth more than any amount of reassurance afterwards.

More than one prize

Use elimination mode and click Hide choice after each spin. The winner leaves the wheel, so the second prize goes to a different person without re-typing the list, and nobody wins twice by accident.

Decide the order before the first spin. Does the first name drawn get the best prize, or the last? Both are defensible; switching halfway through is not.

If prizes differ in value, draw for the biggest one first and work down. It is the order people expect, and expectation is most of what "fair" means in a small draw.

Mistakes that turn a draw into an argument

  • Adding entries after the draw because "they asked just before". Close entries at a stated time and keep it closed.
  • Combining every prize into one spin, so the same person wins everything.
  • A list nobody saw, with a result announced later.
  • Announcing a winner without the entry count. The count is the evidence.
  • A redraw with no stated reason.
  • Two people running the draw, each with their own list.

Every one of these is fixable in advance, and none of them is fixable afterwards.

When a wheel is the wrong tool

Official draws with legal terms, workplace allocations governed by a policy, or anything where someone may later ask for the process. In those cases use a documented method with a verifiable random source and a written record, and check the rules that apply where you are.

For a club raffle, a team lunch, a stream giveaway, a school fair or picking who goes first, a visible browser wheel is entirely adequate, and better than a hat because everyone can see the list.
