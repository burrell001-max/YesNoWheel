How to Start Indoor Gardening With Your Family

Last updated: November 18, 2026

You do not need a yard. A sunny windowsill, a few cups and some seeds are enough to grow something together, and here is a simple way to start.

Why it works with kids

Growing a plant gives children something they rarely get: a result that takes days or weeks instead of seconds. They learn patience, responsibility and cause and effect, because a plant that is not watered looks different from one that is. It also gets the whole family around one small project with real, edible rewards, which is more motivating than a chore chart.

Fast growers to start with

Choose plants that show progress quickly, so interest does not fade.

  • Green onions (scallions). Place the white root ends of store-bought scallions in a glass with an inch of water, put the glass on a bright windowsill, and change the water every couple of days. New green shoots usually appear within days.
  • Bean seeds. Beans sprout fast. Wrap a seed in a damp paper towel, put it in a clear bag, and watch the root and shoot emerge in a few days. Then plant it in soil.
  • Lettuce and salad greens. Many leaf varieties are ready to pick in three to six weeks, and baby leaves can be cut earlier. Sow thickly in a shallow tray.
  • Basil. Loves warmth and at least six hours of light. Pinching off the top leaves encourages it to grow bushier.
  • Mint and chives. Both grow steadily in small pots and forgive uneven watering.

Set it up in 20 minutes

  1. Pick a spot. A south- or west-facing window gives the most light. If you do not have one, a small grow light on a timer works.
  2. Choose containers with drainage. Cups with holes poked in the bottom work, with a saucer underneath. Standing water causes rot.
  3. Use potting mix. Garden soil is too heavy for pots and may carry pests.
  4. Plant and label. Follow the depth on the seed packet. Let the kids write the plant names on craft sticks.
  5. Water gently. Moisten the soil and keep it damp, not soggy, until seedlings appear.

Share the jobs

Give everyone a role. One person waters, one measures growth with a ruler, one keeps a growth journal with drawings or photos, and one decides what to harvest. Rotate weekly so no one feels stuck with the dull job. A simple chart on the fridge helps, and children enjoy checking off "Watered" each morning.

Keep it safe and tidy

  • Wash hands after handling soil and before eating anything you grow. Rinse harvested herbs and greens.
  • Raw sprouts, including bean and alfalfa sprouts, can carry bacteria. Food-safety agencies advise that young children, pregnant people, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system should cook sprouts or avoid eating them raw. Growing a bean for fun and then planting it is fine.
  • If you have pets, check which plants are toxic. Mint, for example, can upset cats and dogs if they eat a lot of it, while basil is generally considered pet-safe. The ASPCA publishes a searchable list.
  • Put pots on a tray or washable mat to catch spills.

When things go wrong

Seedlings that are tall, thin and pale usually need more light. Leaves that turn yellow and soft usually mean too much water. If something dies, treat it as part of the lesson: ask what the plant needed, and try again. Plants are forgiving, and a failed experiment is still a good conversation.

Make it a habit

Pick one plant to start with this week, and add another every few weeks as you get comfortable. Within a couple of months you will have a small windowsill garden, a handful of fresh herbs for dinner, and a family project that keeps going.
