How to Build a Shortlist the Wheel Can Actually Decide

Last updated: November 25, 2026

By Kent B. Williams

A wheel rarely fails because it is random. It fails because the list was never a fair question. Six rules turn a vague set of options into a shortlist that produces an answer you will actually take.

Try it

Open the custom picker wheel. Paste the list in one option per line, then compare how it feels at six entries and at twenty.

Why most wheel lists do not work

Three things go wrong again and again. The options sit at different levels, so they cannot be compared. The options cost wildly different amounts of time or money, so the draw is not between equals. Or the list is padded with things nobody would accept, which turns a random pick into a lottery with a forfeit ticket.

None of these are the wheel's fault, and none of them are fixed by spinning again. They are fixed before the wheel turns.

Rule 1: Keep every option at the same level

A working list holds answers to one question. "Cook", "order Thai", "go to the pub" look like three options, but one is an activity, one is a cuisine and one is a place. That is two decisions pretending to be one.

Split it: eat in or eat out, then which. The same applies to a weekend ("stay home", "the coast", "see my sister"), a project ("write the outline", "edit chapter two", "make a cover") and a shopping list.

A quick test: cover the labels and ask whether these are all the same kind of thing. If one is a category and another is a specific item, you have two wheels stacked on top of each other.

Rule 2: Three to seven options

Two options is a coin flip, and the yes or no wheel does that better with a single spin. Eight or more, and the wheel stops narrowing anything: you cannot hold the slices in your head, so you cannot check that the list is fair, and the result feels arbitrary rather than decisive.

Three to seven keeps every option visible at once, which matters more than it sounds. The whole point of a wheel is that you can see the entire choice in front of you. Once you cannot, you are just generating a random answer to a question you have not finished asking.

If you have more than seven candidates, the work is elimination, not spinning. Cut to seven first, out loud, with everyone watching.

Rule 3: Write options you can check

"Something Asian" is not an option. "The noodle place on 5th with the $12 lunch" is. A concrete option can be checked against your constraints, budgeted, and acted on the moment it wins. A vague one means the decision carries on after the spin, which defeats the point.

  • "A film" becomes "the 8pm showing at the cinema on the corner".
  • "Takeout" becomes "the Thai place that delivers in 30 minutes".
  • "A walk" becomes "the 40-minute loop by the river".
  • "Something with the kids" becomes "the park with the zip line, one hour".
  • "Tidy up" becomes "clear the desk and the shelf above it".

Specific wording also makes the result easier to accept. Nobody argues about whether they wanted "something Asian"; people do argue about whether they wanted that particular restaurant.

Rule 4: Match the commitment

Options should cost roughly the same in time, money and effort. A ten-minute snack and a three-hour dinner are not two answers to one question, and a draw between them is not fair to either.

Either group options by weight, or split the decision: how much time do I have, then which of these fits. The second is usually the honest version of the question you were actually asking.

In practice, a film and a series episode are comparable; a film and a five-minute video are not. A walk round the block and a day trip are not. Group them, then draw inside the group you can actually afford tonight.

Rule 5: Spend the vetoes before the spin

Every group has a veto. The only question is whether it gets used before the wheel turns or after, and the difference matters. A veto after the result is a respin with extra steps, and it teaches everyone that the spin does not count.

A version that works with four or more people: everyone names two options, anyone may strike one option that somebody else named, and whatever survives goes on the wheel. The vetoes are spent, the list is smaller, and nobody can complain about the pool afterwards.

With two people: one veto each, or none. Write the rule down if you are the type of household that renegotiates.

Rule 6: Build the list before you need it

The worst moment to assemble a shortlist is when you are hungry, tired or late. Build the lists when nobody needs them: Friday dinners, weekend outings, rainy-day activities, the films you keep meaning to watch.

Save the entries in the picker and copy the share link into the group chat or onto the kitchen tablet. Next time, the argument starts one step later, because the shortlist is already agreed.

The hidden benefit is that a saved list settles the meta-decision. Nobody has to relitigate which options count, which is usually where the twenty minutes went.

A worked example

Four people, Friday dinner, 7pm, $20 each, one vegetarian, and nobody wants to travel more than fifteen minutes.

  • Constraints: budget, dietary, travel time, start time.
  • Pool: each person names two places, so eight candidates.
  • Cut: three break a constraint, one is closed on Fridays. Four remain.
  • Wording: each of the four is a named place with a rough price and a travel time.
  • Vetoes: one each, spent now. One person uses theirs, so three remain.
  • Spin once, then go.

If the result gets a groan from the person who suggested it, that is useful information. Either the option should not have been in the pool, or the groan was a preference surfacing late. Both point at the list, not at the wheel.

The two-minute version

  • Name the constraint: time, money, travel, dietary, weather.
  • Each person adds two options.
  • Delete anything that breaks a constraint.
  • If more than seven remain, delete the weakest until seven.
  • Agree that vetoes are spent.
  • Spin once and start.

That is the whole method. The wheel is the last five seconds of a process that mostly happens before it.
