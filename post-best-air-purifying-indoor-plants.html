What Are the Best Air-Purifying Indoor Plants for Your Home?

Last updated: November 18, 2026

Plants clean only tiny amounts of air, but they are good company and easy to keep alive. Here is what the science says and five tough plants worth buying.

The honest science

You have probably read that houseplants scrub toxins from the air. That idea comes largely from a 1989 NASA study led by B. C. Wolverton, which measured how some plants removed certain chemicals from small sealed chambers. The results were real, but a sealed lab chamber is very different from a living room where windows open, doors close and air constantly moves.

Later researchers reviewed these studies. A 2019 review by Cummings and Waring found that, in realistic homes and offices, plants remove pollutants far too slowly to matter compared with ordinary ventilation. Getting a measurable effect would take far more plants than any home has room for. So plants are not a replacement for fresh air or an air purifier.

That does not make them pointless. People report that plants make a space feel calmer and more pleasant, and looking after something living gives your day a small routine. Those are good enough reasons to keep a few.

Five that are hard to kill

If you are new to plants, start with ones that forgive mistakes.

Snake plant

Upright, sculptural leaves that tolerate low light and irregular watering. Water every two to three weeks and let the soil dry out in between. Overwatering is the most common way to lose one.

Pothos

A trailing vine that grows in almost any light. Water when the top inch of soil is dry. If a vine gets long and thin, trim it and root the cutting in a glass of water for a free new plant.

Spider plant

Arching striped leaves and small "babies" that you can pot up and share. It likes bright, indirect light and even moisture. Brown tips usually mean the water has a lot of minerals or the plant has gone too dry; rainwater or filtered water can help.

ZZ plant

Glossy, dark leaves and thick underground stems that store water, so it tolerates neglect. Water every two to four weeks. It copes with low light but grows more slowly there.

Peace lily

One of the few easy plants that flowers indoors. It droops dramatically when thirsty and perks up soon after watering, so it tells you what it needs. Keep it out of direct sun.

Keep pets and kids in mind

Several popular houseplants are mildly or moderately toxic if chewed. Pothos, peace lily, ZZ plant and, to a lesser degree, snake plant can irritate the mouth and stomach of cats and dogs. The spider plant is listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs, and Boston fern and parlor palm are also common pet-safe choices. If you share your home with curious animals or toddlers, check each plant against the ASPCA toxic and non-toxic plant list before you buy, and place anything questionable out of reach.

Light, water and pots

Most plant deaths come from too much water, not too little. Use a pot with a drainage hole, water thoroughly, and let the top inch or two of soil dry before the next watering. Push a finger into the soil rather than following a fixed schedule, because light, season and pot size all change how fast it dries. A south or west window gives the most light, while an east window is gentler. Rotate pots every week or two so the plant grows evenly.

If you want cleaner air, do these first

For real improvements, ventilation and source control matter far more than foliage. Open windows when outdoor air is good, use the kitchen fan when you cook, avoid smoking indoors, and choose low-emission cleaning products and paints. If you live somewhere with wildfire smoke, pollen or heavy traffic, a HEPA air purifier sized for the room will do much more than any plant.

A simple plan

Pick two plants that match your light and your pets. Give them a spot you pass every day so you notice how they look. Water by touch, not by calendar. In a month you will know what each one likes, and you can add a third. Plants are slow, gentle company, and that is the real payoff.

Sources: Wolverton et al., "Interior Landscape Plants for Indoor Air Pollution Abatement," NASA, 1989. Cummings and Waring, "Potted plants do not improve indoor air quality: a review and analysis of reported VOC removal efficiencies," Journal of Exposure Science and Environmental Epidemiology, 2019. ASPCA toxic and non-toxic plant list at aspca.org.
