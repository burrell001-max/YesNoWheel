Is Plant-Based Meat Better for You and the Planet?

Last updated: November 18, 2026

Plant-based meat has a lighter footprint than beef, but the health picture depends on the product. Here is what to check on the label.

What it is

Plant-based meat is food made to look, cook and taste like meat without animal ingredients. Most products are built from a protein source such as pea, soy or wheat, combined with fats like coconut, sunflower or canola oil, plus binders, flavorings and sometimes colorings. Some use a plant-made version of heme, the molecule that gives meat its savory flavor. The result is a burger, sausage or mince that behaves a lot like the real thing in the pan.

"Better" depends on what you are comparing and why. Better for the planet, for your heart, for your budget and for your taste buds can each have a different answer.

The environment

Life-cycle studies generally find that plant-based meat products produce fewer greenhouse gas emissions and use less land and water than beef. Beef is a particularly resource-heavy food, so the gap is often large. Against chicken or pork the difference is smaller, and results vary by product, ingredients and how the analysis is done.

It is also worth remembering that minimally processed plant proteins such as beans, lentils, tofu and tempeh typically have an even lower footprint than processed meat alternatives. If lowering your impact is your main goal, those are strong choices, and you can use plant-based meat as a convenience food when you want the familiar format.

Your health

This is where reading the label matters. Compare a plant-based burger with a beef burger of the same size and look at these numbers:

  • Protein. Many plant-based burgers are close to beef, often in the high teens of grams per serving. Check your product.
  • Saturated fat. Coconut oil makes some products as high in saturated fat as lean-to-regular beef. Others are noticeably lower.
  • Sodium. Plant-based products are often higher in sodium than fresh meat, because salt is used for flavor.
  • Fiber. Plant-based options usually contain some fiber, while meat has none.
  • Cholesterol. Plant-based products contain none, as cholesterol comes from animal foods.
  • Ingredient list. A shorter list of recognizable ingredients is a reasonable rule of thumb.

Many plant-based meats are ultra-processed foods. Large observational studies link high intake of ultra-processed foods overall with poorer health outcomes, though those studies do not single out plant-based meat, and they cannot show cause and effect. Small, short trials that swapped animal meat for plant-based meat have reported modest changes in some heart-health markers, but the evidence is limited and the products keep changing. A sensible reading is that these foods can fit in a balanced diet, but they are not automatically health foods.

Taste, cost and convenience

Taste is personal. Some people cannot tell the difference in a sauce-heavy dish like chili or tacos, and others prefer to keep real meat for a grilled burger. Plant-based products often cost more per pound than ground beef, though they can be cheaper than premium cuts. Pan-frying, grilling and baking all work, but cook them according to the package, since they brown and dry out differently from beef.

A flexible approach

You do not need to choose a side. A few easy ways to try this without overhauling your diet:

  • Swap one meat meal a week for a plant-based one, and keep the version you enjoy most.
  • Use beans, lentils or mushrooms in half the meat in chili, bolognese or burgers for a blended version.
  • Buy two different brands and do a blind taste test with your household.
  • Read the label each time, as recipes change often.

If you have a health condition, such as high blood pressure or high cholesterol, or you are making large changes to your diet, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian who can look at your whole eating pattern.
